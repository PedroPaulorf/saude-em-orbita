# Evidence behind Health7's candidate differentiators (NASA Space Apps pitch)

Research date: 2026-11-14. Labels used throughout:
- **[STRONG]**: peer-reviewed primary study, official regulatory text, or official NASA/NOAA statement, read directly or confirmed in more than one source.
- **[MODERATE]**: peer-reviewed but small/observational, or official source seen only through search summary or press release.
- **[WEAK]**: single secondary source, press coverage, anecdote, or unverified figure.
- **[INFERENCE]**: our reasoning from the cited findings, not a finding itself.

---

## 1. Personal baselines / n-of-1 (feature a): does within-person deviation detect change better than population ranges?

### Takeaway
There is solid evidence that physiological signals vary much more *between* people than *within* a person, and that deviation from a person's own baseline (especially resting heart rate) can flag illness that population ranges would miss. But the published detection results are retrospective, non-specific (they flag "something changed", not a diagnosis), and come from terrestrial cohorts. Health7 can defensibly say "personal baseline is the approach used in leading wearable research"; it cannot say it "detects disease earlier".

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG] Lab-medicine principle:** Population reference intervals have significant limitations when interpreting an individual's results; personalized reference intervals built from a person's own prior steady-state results plus biological-variation data are proposed as the alternative. The "index of individuality" (within-person CV / between-person CV) is low for many common analytes, meaning a value can be abnormal *for that person* while still inside the population range. — [Biochemia Medica 2025 case study](https://biochemia-medica.com/en/journal/35/3/10.11613/BM.2025.030901); [Coskun et al., Clin Chem](https://doi.org/10.1093/clinchem/hvaa233); [PMC6301185](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6301185)
- **[MODERATE]** An annual biological-variation study in healthy adults found personal reference intervals covered only a small part of the population interval (reference range index < 0.5 for 8 analytes). — [CCLM 2021](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/cclm-2021-0479/html)
- **[MODERATE]** Modelling suggests ≥3 prior results in steady state are needed for robust personal reference intervals. — [PubMed 35579539](https://www.pubmed.gov/35579539/) (as summarized in search results; not read in full)
- **[MODERATE] Stanford wearables study (Li, Dunn, Snyder et al., PLOS Biology 2017):** ~2 billion measurements from 60 people; the team established each participant's personal baseline range and tracked departures from it, linking them to illness/inflammation. — [Stanford Medicine news](https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2017/01/wearable-sensors-can-tell-when-you-are-getting-sick.html); [paper DOI](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.2001402)
- **[WEAK, anecdotal]** The often-told Lyme disease detection was in one person (senior author Michael Snyder); it is an n=1 observation, not a validated detection rate. — [Stanford Medicine news](https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2017/01/wearable-sensors-can-tell-when-you-are-getting-sick.html)
- **[MODERATE] COVID-19 via resting heart rate deviation (Mishra, Snyder et al., Nat Biomed Eng 2020):** of 32 infected individuals (from ~5,300 participants), 26 (81%) had changes in heart rate, steps or sleep; retrospectively, 63% could have been detected before symptom onset in real time with a two-tier alert based on elevations of resting heart rate *relative to the person's own baseline*. Snyder noted the algorithm cannot distinguish COVID-19 from other infections. — [Stanford Medicine news](https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2020/12/smartwatch-can-detect-early-signs-of-illness.html); [paper DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41551-020-00640-6)
- **[MODERATE]** A follow-up showed a one-class SVM outperformed Mishra's Mahalanobis-distance method on the same type of data, i.e., the specific algorithm matters and is still being refined. — [PMC9097889](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9097889/)
- **[MODERATE] Counter-evidence / caveat (Scripps DETECT, Quer et al., Nature Medicine 2020):** combining wearable data with symptoms beat symptoms alone (AUC 0.80 vs 0.71 per summaries), but resting-heart-rate changes >2 SD were seen in only 30.3% of positive cases, too weak to discriminate on its own. — [Scripps press release](https://www.scripps.edu/news-and-events/press-room/2020/20201029-topol-radin-quer-detect.html); [paper DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-020-1123-x)
- **[MODERATE]** In a later DETECT analysis (875 symptomatic participants), resting heart rate of COVID-positive people took on average 79 days to return to personal baseline; ~14% stayed >5 bpm above baseline for >133 days. Shows baselines themselves can shift after an event. — [Scripps 2021 press release](https://www.scripps.edu/news-and-events/press-room/2021/20210707-radin-longcovid.html)
- **[MODERATE] NASA Twins Study (Garrett-Bakelman et al., Science 2019):** multi-omic and physiological comparison of Scott Kelly (1 year on ISS) vs his identical twin on Earth, plus Scott's own pre-flight data; found gene-expression, telomere, carotid/retinal, microbiome and cognitive changes. Investigators stressed it is "an n of one". — [Cornell Chronicle](https://news.cornell.edu/node/309886); [GeekWire (Feinberg quote)](https://www.geekwire.com/2019/full-findings-nasa-twins-study-raise-questions-long-stints-space/); [paper DOI](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aau8650)
- **[MODERATE] Alerts from consumer wearables produce many false positives:** in a Mayo Clinic records review, only 30/264 (11.4%) patients who sought care after an Apple Watch alert got a cardiovascular diagnosis. — [MobiHealthNews](https://www.mobihealthnews.com/news/apple-watchs-abnormal-pulse-feature-driving-many-unnecessary-healthcare-visits-mayo-clinic)
- **[STRONG, astronaut-specific]** On the ISS, NASA's Reaction Self Test was "algorithmically optimized for astronauts based on normative data on astronauts working in analog environments", and an analysis found a participant's *own past performance* was among the most important predictors of alertness, supporting individual-referenced interpretation in spaceflight. — [NTRS 20140000075](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20140000075/downloads/20140000075.pdf); [NTRS 20210022966](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20210022966) (via search summary)

### Inferences
- [INFERENCE] The strongest defensible claim: "Health7 compares each reading to the astronaut's own pre-mission baseline, the same principle used in Stanford/Scripps wearable research and in personalized laboratory reference intervals, because variation between people is often larger than variation within a person."
- [INFERENCE] Spaceflight itself shifts physiology (fluid shifts, deconditioning, Twins Study changes), so a pre-mission baseline may become "stale" in flight. Health7 should present baseline deviation as "change to review", and could mention an in-flight rolling baseline as future work.
- [INFERENCE] Personal-baseline alerts trade population-range false alarms for a different false-alarm profile; the Mayo data shows alerts drive workups and anxiety. Pairing personal baselines with transparent rules (feature b) is the logical mitigation.
- **Health7 should NOT claim:** that it detects illness/infection earlier; that personal baselines are "clinically validated" for astronauts; any sensitivity/specificity number; that it reproduces Snyder/iPOP or Twins Study omics methods (it uses simple vital/self-report deviations); that its baseline window size is evidence-based (≥3 steady-state readings is a lab-medicine guideline, not a vital-signs rule).

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve primary sources for the Stanford iPOP (integrative personal omics profiling) cohort, so it is not cited here.
- No head-to-head study was found that directly compares sensitivity/specificity of personal-baseline vs population-threshold alerts for vital signs in astronauts.
- The precise ISS RST normative/individual scoring method was seen only through NTRS summaries.

## 2. Communication delay and medical autonomy (feature d)

### Takeaway
NASA officially states that Mars-class missions will have no real-time communication, communication blackouts, and no evacuation, and is developing "Earth-Independent Medical Operations" (EIMO), including onboard decision support. Health7's offline-first design with a queued report for the ground flight surgeon matches this concept of operations well. Use NASA's own figure for Mars delay (about 4–24 minutes one way), not "22 min".

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG] NASA SCaN:** "At Mars' closest approach — about 35 million miles away — the delay is about four minutes"; at greatest distance (~250 million miles) "the delay is around 24 minutes"; astronauts "would need to wait between four and 24 minutes for their messages to reach mission control." For spacecraft close to Earth, latency "is almost negligible." — [NASA, Space Communications: 7 Things You Need to Know](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/tech-demonstration/space-communications-7-things-you-need-to-know)
- **[MODERATE]** Other sources cite ~3–22 minutes one way, depending on orbital geometry, so figures differ by source. — [search-result summary of multiple sources incl. NTRS 20190032318](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20190032318/downloads/20190032318.pdf)
- **[STRONG] Solar conjunction:** about every two years Mars passes behind the Sun from Earth's view; NASA pauses commanding for roughly two weeks (e.g., 2–16 Oct 2021) because solar plasma can corrupt radio signals. — [Space.com 2021](https://www.space.com/nasa-mars-blackout-solar-conjunction-2021); [Space.com 2015](https://www.space.com/29576-nasa-mars-spacecraft-radio-blackout.html)
- **[STRONG] NASA EIMO ConOps (HRP ExMC, 13 Feb 2024):** aims to "Monitor and maintain crew health and performance during communication delays and in an environment that does not allow emergency evacuation or terrestrial medical assistance." Contrasts ISS ("Real-time communications", "Emergency evacuations possible", "Limited onboard medical care (Earth-reliant)") with exploration missions ("No real-time communications + blackouts", "No evacuations possible", "Expanded onboard medical care (crew/vehicle-reliant)"), 650 to >900-day missions. Defines EIMO as "The gradual transition of medical care and decision making from terrestrial to space-based assets." — [NTRS 20240001402 (PDF)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240001402/downloads/2024_IWS_EIMO_ConOps_FINAL.pdf)
- **[STRONG]** Same ConOps: acute decisions face "Communication latency and reduced data transmission bandwidth"; prolonged (non-emergent) condition management should be "less dependent on real time ground support" with "access to on-board CDSS"; and "Delays / lack of ground support = increased task demand, task switching & higher cognitive load (all shown to impair decision making)", so tasks that can be automated should be identified, including "storage & retrieval of medical record information". — [NTRS 20240001402](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240001402/downloads/2024_IWS_EIMO_ConOps_FINAL.pdf)
- **[STRONG]** NASA's Autonomous Medical Operations (AMO) project "is developing an on-board software-based Medical Decision Support System (MDSS)"; the Crew Health and Performance Integrated Data Architecture (CHP-IDA) is a platform to combine crew-relevant data sources. — [NTRS 20240001402](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240001402/downloads/2024_IWS_EIMO_ConOps_FINAL.pdf)
- **[MODERATE]** A 2025 NASA presentation describes an AI-supported system helping the Crew Medical Officer when ground support is unavailable or too delayed. — [NTRS 20250000009](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20250000009) (search summary only)
- **[MODERATE]** Canadian Space Agency frames remote healthcare in space and on Earth as a shared problem requiring autonomy. — [CSA](https://www.asc-csa.gc.ca/eng/health/remote-healthcare-in-space-and-on-earth.asp)

### Inferences
- [INFERENCE] Moon one-way light time ≈ 384,400 km / 299,792 km/s ≈ 1.28 s (commonly rounded to 1.3 s). I did not find a nasa.gov page stating 1.3 s directly; present it as a physics calculation, and note that for the Moon the delay is minor and the real constraints are coverage gaps and bandwidth.
- [INFERENCE] Health7's "report queued for downlink" mirrors the store-and-forward pattern implied by the ConOps (reduced bandwidth, ground teams no longer in the loop in real time). It is fair to say Health7 "models" Earth-independent operations; it is a toy model of one small piece (routine check-ins), not of EIMO.
- **Health7 should NOT claim:** to be part of / compatible with NASA EIMO, AMO, MDSS or CHP-IDA; that it replaces the flight surgeon or CMO; that delay is a fixed "22 minutes" (say "about 4 to 24 minutes one way, per NASA"); that conjunction blackouts always last exactly two weeks.

### Gaps
- No official NASA page found stating the 1.3 s lunar delay.
- Mars surface-mission comms concept (relay satellites, expected bandwidth) not researched.
- Exact conjunction dates for future crewed Mars windows not checked.

## 3. Explainable / transparent alerts (feature b)

### Takeaway
Regulators explicitly value transparency: the current FDA CDS guidance (issued 29 Jan 2026, superseding the 2022 and 6 Jan 2026 versions) says non-device CDS must let a *health care professional* independently review the basis of recommendations, with plain-language inputs and logic. Alert fatigue is well documented (override rates 46–96%). However, explanations are not a cure-all: a randomized JAMA study found explanations did not help clinicians catch a biased model. Health7's "show the rule in plain words + Demonstration rule label" is consistent with the regulatory spirit and honest, but does not make it a regulated or non-device CDS.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG] FDA version:** "Clinical Decision Support Software... Document issued on January 29, 2026. This document supersedes 'Clinical Decision Support Software' issued on January 6, 2026." (The 2022 version is no longer current.) — [FDA CDS guidance PDF](https://www.fda.gov/media/109618/download)
- **[STRONG] Criterion 4 text:** software must be "intended for the purpose of enabling such health care professional to independently review the basis for such recommendations... so that it is not the intent that such health care professional rely primarily on any of such recommendations." All four criteria must be met. — [FDA CDS guidance](https://www.fda.gov/media/109618/download)
- **[STRONG] What FDA recommends for Criterion 4:** (a) state intended use, HCP user and patient population; time-critical tasks do not meet Criterion 4; (b) identify required inputs "with plain language instructions on how the inputs should be obtained, their relevance, and data quality requirements"; (c) a "plain language description of the underlying algorithm development and validation"; (d) output "relevant patient-specific information and other knowns/unknowns for consideration (e.g., missing, corrupted, or unexpected input data values)". Information should be presented in a way that "avoids information overload, including prioritizing the most decision-relevant information". FDA cites automation bias as the rationale. — [FDA CDS guidance](https://www.fda.gov/media/109618/download)
- **[STRONG] Important limit for Health7:** "Software functions that support or provide recommendations to patients or caregivers — not HCPs — meet the definition of a device." Criterion 1 also excludes software that analyzes "a pattern or signal from a signal acquisition system". — [FDA CDS guidance](https://www.fda.gov/media/109618/download)
- **[MODERATE] Alert fatigue:** a 2020 systematic review of 23 studies reported average CDS alert override rates of 46.2%–96.2%. — [JMIR Med Inform 2020](https://medinform.jmir.org/2020/7/e15653/)
- **[MODERATE]** A 2019 JAMIA systematic review found tailoring alerts to the clinical role was the most successful method to reduce alert fatigue. — [AHRQ PSNet summary](https://psnet.ahrq.gov/issue/medication-safety-alert-fatigue-may-be-reduced-interaction-design-and-clinical-role-tailoring)
- **[WEAK]** A 2025 preprint meta-analysis pooled a drug-interaction alert override rate of ~79.8%; measurement of alert fatigue is inconsistent across studies. — [JMIR preprint](https://preprints.jmir.org/preprint/88578); [MetroHealth 2026 review](https://www.metrohealth.org/globalassets/metrohealth-documents/population-health-research-institute/ray-wilson-et-al-2026-alert-fatigue-systematic-review.pdf)
- **[STRONG] Counter-evidence (Jabbour et al., JAMA 2023, randomized vignette study, 457 hospitalist clinicians):** baseline accuracy 73.0%; a standard AI model raised accuracy by 2.9 points (4.4 with explanations); a systematically biased model *lowered* accuracy by 11.3 points, and by 9.1 points even with explanations, i.e., explanations did not help clinicians recognize a biased model. — [Michigan Medicine](https://www.michiganmedicine.org/health-lab/clinicians-could-be-fooled-biased-ai-despite-explanations); [MDedge](https://mdedge.com/content/systemic-bias-ai-models-may-undermine-diagnostic-accuracy)
- **[WEAK]** A Johns Hopkins experiment with 220 doctors reported local explanations increased accuracy and speed but also increased trust in the AI regardless of correctness. — [JHU CS news](https://www.cs.jhu.edu/?p=1985623) (secondary; primary not read)

### Inferences
- [INFERENCE] Health7's rules are simple, human-readable thresholds on deviation from baseline, not image heatmaps or ML. "Show the rule" is closer to FDA's "plain language description of the logic" and "patient-specific information" than to post-hoc ML explanations, so the Jabbour caveat applies less, but it still means: transparency does not guarantee correctness, and a wrong rule shown clearly is still wrong. That is exactly why labeling rules "Demonstration rule" (not clinical) is the honest choice.
- [INFERENCE] Showing missing/unexpected inputs (e.g., "no baseline yet", "reading outside sensor range") would align with FDA recommendation (d) and is a cheap improvement.
- **Health7 should NOT claim:** to be FDA-compliant, FDA-cleared, or to "meet the FDA non-device CDS criteria" (the crew-facing view targets non-HCPs, and wearable signal analysis may fall under Criterion 1, so it would likely be a device if used clinically); that explainability "eliminates alert fatigue" or "makes the system safe"; that its demonstration thresholds are clinical thresholds. Safe phrasing: "inspired by FDA's transparency principle that users should be able to review the basis of a recommendation."

### Gaps
- No study found that tests plain-language rule display (as opposed to ML explanations) on override rates or trust.
- I did not verify the Nature Medicine dermatology XAI study mentioned in secondary sources; omitted.

## 4. Behavioral health in isolation, brief self-report and PVT (feature c)

### Takeaway
Sleep loss, stress and fatigue are documented in real ISS astronauts, and the ISS already uses a short, self-administered 3-minute PVT (Reaction Self Test, RST) plus brief visual-analog self-ratings of stress, fatigue, sleepiness and workload. Mars-500 showed large *individual* differences (one crewmember with depressive symptoms most weeks, others unaffected). Short-duration HERA isolation showed little cognitive decline. This strongly supports a separate, brief behavioral check-in, and supports *not* producing a single score.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG] ISS sleep and RST (Jones, Basner, Mollicone, Mott, Dinges, Sleep 2022):** 24 astronauts on 6-month ISS missions (2009–2014), 2,856 RST sessions. Astronauts averaged ~6.5 h sleep in flight and used the full 8.5-h opportunity on only 5.9% of nights; ≤6 h sleep was associated with slower PVT-B response speed, higher stress and workload; <5 h with more fatigue/sleepiness/exhaustion; stress ratings rose across mission quarters. — [PMC8919197](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8919197)
- **[STRONG] RST protocol:** 3-minute PVT-B, given morning and evening; in flight every fourth day plus extra sessions around sleep shifts and spacewalks; lapses defined as RT ≥355 ms. Self-ratings on visual analog scales included sleep quality, workload, stress, mental fatigue, physical exhaustion, tiredness, sleepiness, plus caffeine use. Authors' limitations: self-reported sleep, observational design, no continuous sleep-wake data. — [PMC8919197](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8919197)
- **[MODERATE]** NASA renamed the PVT-B "Reaction Self Test" for ISS; used since 2009; low-mass, self-administered; developed with Basner and Dinges (Penn). When it shows reduced alertness it is "a warning sign indicating caution". — [NTRS 20140000075](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20140000075/downloads/20140000075.pdf); [Penn Astronaut Behavioral Health](https://www.med.upenn.edu/uep/astronaut.html); [NASA Spinoff 2013](https://spinoff.nasa.gov/Spinoff2013/hm_1.html)
- **[MODERATE]** A later analysis found past performance, reported fatigue and stress, and temperature and radiation exposure were among the most important variables associated with alertness. — [NTRS 20210022966](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20210022966) (search summary)
- **[STRONG] Mars-500 (Basner et al., PNAS 2013):** 520-day confinement; crew became progressively more sedentary (hypokinesis) and showed altered sleep duration and timing; one crewmember drifted to a ~25-h day. — [PNAS DOI](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1212646110); [Scientific American blog](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/basic-space/what-a-fake-mars-mission-says-about-our-own-sleep-habits/)
- **[STRONG] Mars-500 psychology (Basner et al., PLoS ONE 2014):** 6 men, 520 days, weekly questionnaires plus vigilance test and actigraphy. One crewmember reported depressive symptoms in 93% of weeks (mild-to-moderate in >10%); one developed persistent sleep-onset insomnia; two showed no disturbance. Conflicts with mission control were reported 5x more than among crew. — [PLoS ONE DOI](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0093298)
- **[MODERATE] HERA (Frontiers in Physiology 2020):** confinement/isolation up to 2 weeks did not significantly impair cognitive performance, but authors caution workload, sleep restriction and circadian disruption may matter on longer missions. — [Frontiers in Physiology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2020.00394/full)
- **[MODERATE] Antarctica:** a NASA HRP presentation cites Palinkas (1992): 51.5% of winter-over personnel reported difficulty with concentration or memory, and cites neurostructural (hippocampal) changes after winter-over. NASA notes HERA lacks microgravity, threat to life and long duration, and "there is no true analog for time." — [NTRS 20230015299](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230015299/downloads/Extr%20Envnmnts-NASA-HRP%20resch.pdf); [NTRS 20240010442](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240010442/downloads/Behavioral%20Challenges%20of%20a%20Mars%20Mission.pdf)
- **[MODERATE]** Basner's group also developed the Cognition battery, part of NASA's Behavioral Health and Performance Standard Measures. — [Penn Astronaut Behavioral Health](https://www.med.upenn.edu/uep/astronaut.html)

### Inferences
- [INFERENCE] Health7's mood/stress/rest self-report directly parallels the RST's VAS self-ratings, and its reaction test parallels the RST/PVT-B. Defensible claim: "inspired by the ISS Reaction Self Test (a 3-min PVT used on ISS since 2009)."
- [INFERENCE] Mars-500's strong individual differences support both the personal-baseline approach and the choice of *no aggregate wellbeing score* (a score would hide that one person is struggling on one dimension).
- [INFERENCE] A browser/phone reaction test has device latency and input-lag variability (tens of ms) that a 355-ms lapse threshold is sensitive to; therefore Health7 should compare to the user's own baseline on the same device and not report "lapses" as validated.
- **Health7 should NOT claim:** that its test *is* the PVT or RST, or is validated; that it diagnoses depression, fatigue, or fitness for duty; that self-report is objective; that its timing matches PVT standards (3 min / 10 min). Do not cite HERA as evidence of deterioration (it found none at 2 weeks).

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve a validation study comparing short (e.g., <3 min) smartphone PVTs to the standard 10-min PVT; worth one more search if the team shortens the test.
- No primary source retrieved for validity of single-item mood/stress scales.

## 5. Space weather and radiation awareness (feature e)

### Takeaway
Solar energetic particle (SEP) events are the main acute radiation hazard beyond Earth's magnetosphere, and NASA monitors them operationally through SRAG (JSC), the Moon to Mars Space Weather Analysis Office (Goddard), NOAA SWPC forecasts, and onboard dosimeters with tiered alarm thresholds. DONKI is a NASA research catalog that explicitly says it is "prototyping quality", and that NOAA SWPC is the official US source. Health7's framing ("awareness, not dose") is correct and should stay that way. Note: DONKI web/API URLs changed on 30 Sept 2026.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG] SEP hazard:** SEP events accelerate particles to near light speed and "could raise radiation levels inside the spacecraft"; cumulative exposure can "contribute to increased risks of developing cancer" and disorders affecting cognition and performance. — [NASA Science, Artemis II space weather](https://science.nasa.gov/missions/artemis/artemis-2/to-protect-artemis-ii-astronauts-nasa-experts-keep-eyes-on-sun/)
- **[STRONG] Operational chain:** M2M SWAO (Goddard) "continuously assesses solar activity" and shares analysis with SRAG at JSC; NASA and NOAA "will monitor the Sun around the clock"; SRAG combines this with NOAA forecasts and in-cabin data to advise flight control. — [NASA Science](https://science.nasa.gov/missions/artemis/artemis-2/to-protect-artemis-ii-astronauts-nasa-experts-keep-eyes-on-sun/)
- **[STRONG] Onboard dose, not forecasts, drives action:** Orion has six HERA radiation sensors measuring dose rates and crew wear "crew active dosimeters"; a lower dose threshold prompts closer monitoring and coordination with medical/flight teams, a higher one triggers "a recommendation for the crew to take shelter"; crews reconfigure the cabin with stowed mass. — [NASA Science](https://science.nasa.gov/missions/artemis/artemis-2/to-protect-artemis-ii-astronauts-nasa-experts-keep-eyes-on-sun/)
- **[MODERATE]** ESA: flares and small/medium CMEs pose little risk to crewed Moon missions; SEP events "are the ones to watch out for"; especially relevant for EVAs and lunar surface work. — [ESA](https://www.esa.int/Space_Safety/Space_weather/Protecting_Artemis_and_lunar_explorers_from_space_radiation)
- **[MODERATE]** SRAG operators watch real-time data and support Mission Control; NOAA SWPC notifies when events occur; NASA and SWPC have exercised SEP forecast/mitigation procedures. — [NTRS 20230004380 (Whitman, SRAG)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230004380/downloads/BIRA2023_Whitman_v3.pdf); [CCMC ISEP](https://ccmc.gsfc.nasa.gov/ISEP)
- **[MODERATE]** NOAA SWPC proton event threshold: 10 pfu (protons/cm²·s·sr) at ≥10 MeV; S-scale spans 10 to 100,000 pfu. — [SWPC GOES proton flux](https://swpc-drupal.woc.noaa.gov/products/goes-proton-flux)
- **[STRONG] DONKI status:** DONKI is "a comprehensive on-line tool for space weather forecasters, scientists, and the general space science community"; entries from June 2020 onward come primarily from M2M SWAO; data "should be considered only as prototyping quality and in research context"; NOAA SWPC "is the United States Government official source for space weather forecasts"; contents are "preliminary NASA research products." Event types include FLR, SEP, CME, IPS, MPC, GST, RBE, HSS. — [CCMC DONKI tool page](https://ccmc.gsfc.nasa.gov/tools/DONKI); [DONKI Disclaimer](https://kauai.ccmc.gsfc.nasa.gov/DONKI/Disclaimer)
- **[STRONG, operational for the team]** As of 30 Sept 2026, the DONKI web app and API "have migrated to new official URLs"; replacing "kauai.ccmc.gsfc.nasa.gov" with "ccmc.gsfc.nasa.gov" routes directly. — [CCMC DONKI tool page](https://ccmc.gsfc.nasa.gov/tools/DONKI)
- **[WEAK]** A third-party package README says api.nasa.gov/DONKI now returns 301 to ccmc.gsfc.nasa.gov/DONKI-API/get/ without a key. Not confirmed on NASA pages; test before the demo. — [packagist omnilab/donki](https://packagist.org/packages/omnilab/donki)

### Inferences
- [INFERENCE] DONKI SEP/FLR records describe events near Earth/the Sun; they do not measure dose at a crew location, and shielding/location/timing dominate actual dose (NASA itself relies on onboard dosimeters for shelter decisions). So "does not give the received dose" is accurate and should be stated.
- [INFERENCE] Flares (FLR) alone are a weaker radiation signal than SEP; Health7 should weight SEP events more prominently and avoid implying every flare is a crew hazard.
- [INFERENCE] Because DONKI is a research product and its URL just changed, the offline-first design should cache the last successful fetch with a timestamp and show "data may be outdated".
- **Health7 should NOT claim:** to forecast radiation storms, estimate crew dose, or advise sheltering; that DONKI is an official forecast (NOAA SWPC is); that it replicates SRAG. Safe phrasing: "situational awareness of solar events from NASA's DONKI research catalog; official forecasts come from NOAA SWPC and crew dose comes from onboard dosimeters."

### Gaps
- No NASA flight-rule document with numeric shelter thresholds was retrieved (not public in results).
- DONKI's typical latency between event and catalog entry was not found.

## 6. Gamification: rewarding behaviors vs outcomes (feature f)

### Takeaway
Gamification produces small, partly durable increases in health behaviors (best evidence: physical activity), with competition-based designs performing best in one large RCT. Evidence on rewarding *process* (behaviors) vs *outcomes* is mixed for clinical endpoints, but process incentives reliably increase the targeted behavior. The strongest argument for Health7's "reward actions, not results" design is risk avoidance: rewarding measurement results invites gaming/misreporting and anxiety (e.g., orthosomnia), and it would make the health data less trustworthy.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG] STEP UP RCT (Patel et al., JAMA Intern Med 2019):** 602 adults with overweight/obesity, 24-week behavioral-economics game (points/levels for step goals, loss of points for missed days) plus 12-week follow-up. Competition arm: ~920 more steps/day vs control during intervention and ~569 more after the game ended; support and collaboration arms lost significance after the game ended. — [Penn Medicine news](https://www.pennmedicine.org/news/news-releases/2019/september/using-a-wearable-device-to-exercise-more-add-competition-to-improve-your-results); [QxMD abstract](https://read.qxmd.com/read/31498375/effectiveness-of-behaviorally-designed-gamification-interventions-with-social-incentives-for-increasing-physical-activity-among-overweight-and-obese-adults-across-the-united-states-the-step-up-randomized-clinical-trial)
- **[MODERATE] Meta-analysis (Mazeas et al., JMIR 2022, 16 studies):** small-to-medium effect of gamification on physical activity (Hedges g = 0.42) and a smaller effect at ~14-week follow-up (g = 0.15). — [JMIR DOI](https://doi.org/10.2196/26779); [PMC8669581](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8669581)
- **[MODERATE]** Earlier systematic review: 59% of studies positive, 41% mixed; evidence mostly moderate or lower quality. — [Johnson et al. 2016, PMC6096297](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6096297/)
- **[MODERATE]** In depression apps, gamification was not a significant moderator of outcomes. — [JMIR Mental Health 2021](https://mental.jmir.org/2021/11/e32199)
- **[MODERATE] Process vs outcome incentives:** in a cholesterol RCT, neither process-based (statin adherence), outcome-based (LDL target) nor combined incentives improved LDL-C vs control. — [PubMed 34605920](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34605920/). Other trials (diabetes, Medicaid, weight loss) are mixed; process incentives tend to raise targeted behaviors (adherence, attendance) without consistent outcome gains. — [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03139019](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03139019); [PMC8357673](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8357673) (search summary)
- **[STRONG] Motivation risk (Deci, Koestner & Ryan, Psych Bull 1999, 128 experiments):** engagement-, completion- and performance-contingent tangible rewards undermined free-choice intrinsic motivation (d = −0.40, −0.36, −0.28); positive verbal feedback enhanced it (d ≈ 0.33). — [Deci et al. 1999 PDF](https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf)
- **[MODERATE] Measurement-chasing harm:** "Orthosomnia" case series (Baron et al., J Clin Sleep Med 2017): patients' perfectionistic pursuit of ideal tracker sleep data worsened behavior (more time in bed). — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2017/02/170228185322.htm)

### Inferences
- [INFERENCE] For astronauts, health readings feed medical decisions; rewarding "good numbers" would create an incentive to under-report symptoms or re-take readings until they look good, corrupting the data the flight surgeon relies on. Rewarding completion of the routine (check-in done, test done, report sent) keeps the incentive aligned with data completeness, not with looking healthy. This is a reasoned design argument, not an empirically tested one in spaceflight.
- [INFERENCE] Deci et al. suggest informational feedback (e.g., "streak", "you completed all checks") is safer than controlling, tangible-style rewards; Health7 should keep rewards light and informational.
- [INFERENCE] STEP UP's best arm was competition; competition among a small crew could create social pressure, so it is reasonable for Health7 to avoid crew leaderboards.
- **Health7 should NOT claim:** that its gamification improves health outcomes or adherence in astronauts (no evidence in this population); that "rewarding actions not results" is proven superior for clinical outcomes (evidence is mixed); that effects persist long-term. Safe phrasing: "rewards completing the routine, never the values, so there is no incentive to report a 'better' number."
